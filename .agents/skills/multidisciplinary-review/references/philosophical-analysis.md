# Philosophical Analysis Lens

Use this lens when the dispute depends on concepts, foundations, experience,
knowledge, values, or incompatible frames rather than facts alone.

## Modes of Inquiry

- **Conceptual:** Define the key terms, their boundaries, and possible category
  errors. Check whether one word is carrying several meanings.
- **Epistemic:** Ask what can be known, by whom, through which evidence, and
  with what limits. Separate justification from confidence or consensus.
- **Ontological:** Identify what kinds of entities, relations, causes, or levels
  of description the argument assumes.
- **Phenomenological:** Describe the relevant experience or appearance before
  explaining it. Distinguish lived experience from a third-person model.
- **Dialectical:** Formulate the strongest opposing position. Preserve the
  tension until a real contradiction, synthesis, scope distinction, or open
  conflict is established.
- **Normative:** Surface values, duties, rights, distributional effects, and the
  implicit account of what counts as good or acceptable.

## Guardrails

- Do not use a philosopher's name as a substitute for an argument.
- Do not introduce abstract terminology unless it changes the analysis.
- Do not convert a value judgment into a factual claim or a descriptive claim
  into an obligation without an explicit bridge.
- Do not force fundamentally different traditions into superficial agreement.

## Output

State the clarified concepts, hidden commitments, strongest competing frame,
normative choice, and which disagreement can or cannot be settled by evidence.
