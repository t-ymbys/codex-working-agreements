# Literature Review and Novelty

Use this reference for literature surveys, prior-art searches, novelty assessment, attribution, and current research-frontier questions.

## 1. Literature Before Novelty

Do not assume the proposed idea is novel.

Before making a novelty claim:

1. identify the closest research areas;
2. identify terminology variants;
3. search for direct predecessors;
4. search adjacent fields that may formulate the same idea differently;
5. identify contemporary competing methods;
6. inspect important references cited by the closest papers;
7. determine what was actually proved, computed, or demonstrated.

A research gap must be derived from the literature rather than assumed before review.

If the literature invalidates the original gap, revise the research plan.

## 2. External Retrieval Is Mandatory for Literature Claims

Use external retrieval for claims about:

- prior work;
- novelty;
- priority;
- attribution;
- publication status;
- bibliographic details;
- the current research frontier;
- what a specific paper proved or reported.

Suitable sources include:

- original papers;
- arXiv and comparable repositories;
- journal and publisher sites;
- Crossref or DOI metadata;
- bibliographic databases;
- institutional repositories;
- official archives.

Model memory may suggest search hypotheses but is not verification.

If retrieval cannot be completed, mark the result `NOT VERIFIED`.

Never invent missing metadata.

## 3. Source Hierarchy

Prefer sources approximately in this order:

1. original papers containing the relevant result;
2. peer-reviewed journal or conference papers;
3. authoritative monographs or textbooks;
4. official repositories, datasets, standards, or institutional documents;
5. preprints;
6. high-quality secondary literature;
7. informal commentary only when necessary.

Preprints may be important current sources, but identify them as preprints.

Distinguish, when relevant:

- preprint date;
- revision date;
- publication date;
- conference presentation date.

Verify important metadata rather than inferring it.

## 4. Search Strategy

For important questions, search at multiple semantic levels:

### Direct terminology
Search the user's terminology and exact mathematical or methodological terms.

### Terminology variants
Search synonyms, older vocabulary, alternate notation, and adjacent disciplinary language.

### Structural equivalents
Search for the underlying mathematical or conceptual construction even when terminology differs.

### Citation neighborhood
Inspect:

- references cited by the closest papers;
- papers citing the closest papers;
- relevant reviews;
- papers by key authors.

### Competing approaches
Search methods that solve the same problem through different formalism.

The purpose is to discover conceptual duplicates, not merely lexical matches.

## 5. Search Bounds

Before beginning an open-ended search, define:

- target question;
- minimum evidence required;
- reasonable query or source budget;
- conditions that would justify deeper search.

Diversify search terms instead of repeating nearly identical queries.

Stop when:

- predefined depth is reached;
- additional sources are materially redundant;
- the remaining uncertainty cannot be resolved by further ordinary search;
- the next useful step is mathematical, computational, empirical, or human review.

Record residual uncertainty rather than searching indefinitely.

Do not interpret failure to find prior art as proof that none exists.

## 6. Critical Literature Survey

A literature survey is not a bibliography.

Organize prior work around questions such as:

- What research lineage led to the present problem?
- What conceptual families exist?
- Which works are direct competitors?
- Which are adjacent but materially different?
- What assumptions distinguish them?
- What was proved?
- What was only demonstrated numerically or empirically?
- What remains unresolved?

For important papers record, where useful:

- citation;
- central claim;
- assumptions;
- method;
- evidence;
- limitations;
- relevance;
- overlap with the current contribution;
- differences;
- unresolved questions.

Prefer a conceptual taxonomy over a chronological list.

## 7. Claim-Level Novelty Matrix

For substantial projects, construct a claim-level matrix such as:

| Claim / Idea | Existing Literature | Previous Unpublished Work | Current Contribution Candidate | Evidence | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Possible status labels:

- established;
- previously established by the researcher;
- partially known;
- closely related prior art;
- apparently new but not fully verified;
- genuine novelty candidate;
- not yet assessable.

Evaluate novelty at the level of:

- theorem;
- mechanism;
- method;
- representation;
- algorithm;
- experiment;
- dataset;
- benchmark;
- prediction;
- explanatory result.

Do not treat recombination of known components as automatically novel.

If the contribution is a connection between known structures, specify what the connection adds.

## 8. Correctness and Novelty Are Independent

Evaluate separately:

### Correctness
Is the result mathematically, logically, computationally, or empirically valid?

### Novelty
Assuming it is correct, is it substantially new?

Possible outcomes include:

- correct but known;
- new but incorrect;
- partially correct and partially known;
- correct and new but minor;
- correct, new, and significant.

Evidence for one dimension does not establish the other.

## 9. Reporting

A good literature conclusion should state:

- what was searched;
- the strongest prior art found;
- the closest overlap;
- the remaining difference;
- confidence in novelty;
- important unverified areas;
- whether deeper expert review is still needed.

Prefer qualified conclusions such as:

`No direct precedent was found within the performed search`

over absolute claims such as:

`No prior work exists`

unless the stronger statement is genuinely justified.
